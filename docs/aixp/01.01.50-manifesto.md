# Chapter 1 - The Manifesto: Pillars and Rejection Criteria

aiXP is organized around five pillars. Each pillar is paired with a rejection criterion - a test for whether a proposed practice, tool, or workflow belongs in the discipline or contradicts it.

---

## Pillar 1 - Verification is the work

Generating code is not the bottleneck. Writing code, producing output, shipping diffs - these are now cheap. The constrained resource is the human capacity to verify that what was generated is correct, safe, and intentional. Every practice in aiXP either protects or expands that capacity.

**What aiXP is not:** a way to generate more code faster. Volume of output is not a success metric.

**Rejection test:** *Does this practice increase the rate of unverified change, or decrease it?* If a proposed workflow produces more output without a corresponding increase in verification rigor, it fails this test.

> [!NOTE] XP as a Trust Factory
> In [Trust Factory](https://tidyfirst.substack.com/p/trust-factory), Kent Beck explains that classic Extreme Programming's practices (TDD, pairing, CI, small releases) were never just workflow habits; they were engineered as a "trust factory" to produce confidence between developers, code, and customers. In aiXP, Pillar 1 re-aims that trust factory: verification is not an afterthought or a gatekeeping tax, but possibly, the primary and most important work of developer.

---

## Pillar 2 - Judgment cannot be delegated

AI tools can implement, refactor, review, test, and document. They cannot decide what the software should do, judge whether it does it well enough, or be accountable for the result. The human's role is not to review the AI's work as a formality - it is to exercise the judgment the AI cannot. Practices that reduce  or offload that judgment (high velocity rubber-stamp reviews, swarms, unsupervised agents) are not efficiencies, they're failures.

**What aiXP is not:** a methodology for removing humans from the loop. The loop is the point.

**Rejection test:** *Does this practice require the human to exercise real judgment, or does it blindly approve a decision made by AI?* A practice that consistently produces the second outcome has broken this pillar.

---

## Pillar 3 - Contracts govern what, not how

Engineering authority is fractal: it operates on the exact same principle from the executive suite down to the terminal prompt. At every layer of an organization or an agent topology, authority lives strictly at the boundary of intent, constraints, and verifiable outcomes. 

Every tier specifies the *what*: 
- **Executive leadership** defines business outcomes, risk tolerances, and resource limits - not technology stacks or sprint-point velocity.
- **Product and systems architects** define domain invariants, user workflows, and data schemas - not procedural algorithms.
- **Lead Navigators and supervising agents** define discrete task boundaries, interface contracts, and failing acceptance tests (ATDD) - not private unit tests or pseudocode.

The implementing tier - whether an engineering team, an individual practitioner, or an AI Driver - is granted operational freedom over the internal *how*, including its own internal unit-testing loops. Reviewers do not micromanage or audit the implementer's private scaffolding; they hold the boundary strictly at the acceptance threshold.

When work travels back up the chain, it must clear an explicit **acceptance threshold**. Because downstream implementers are not trapped in procedural micromanagement, this return trip creates a vital system of checks and balances. Implementers discover novel language primitives, cleaner functional compositions, or unconsidered edge cases that upstream planners missed. A solution that turns the test suite green through an unexpected mechanism is not an insubordination - it is an independent second opinion that potentially elevates the final product.

Briefing *how-first* (dictating pseudocode, prescribing algorithms, or micromanaging implementation steps) is an organizational and cognitive failure mode. It turns downstream collaborators into an echo chamber, blinding them with the supervisor's assumptions and cascading inherited hallucinations through the system. If an implementation violates an unstated expectation, the failure is in the contract, not the code.

**What aiXP is not:** a hierarchy of procedural micromanagement where supervisors, managers, or humans pseudocode or dictate mechanics while downstream implementers execute blind boilerplate.

**Rejection test:** *Did you reject this implementation because it violated a testable invariant, or because it solved the problem differently than you would have?* If an implementation satisfies all acceptance criteria, honors Simple Design (passes tests, reveals intent, eliminates duplication, minimizes elements), and introduces zero regressions, rejecting it solely because the syntax conflicts with personal habit is ego-driven micromanagement.

---

## Pillar 4 - The discipline scales with the tools

As AI tools become more capable, the temptation is to loosen the discipline - if the model is better, surely the review can be lighter? The argument runs the wrong direction. More capable tools produce more output at higher speed; the discipline has to keep pace with what's being produced, not relax because the producer is impressive. An error generated at high speed and low scrutiny is not less wrong than one written slowly and reviewed carefully.

**What aiXP is not:** a fixed checklist that becomes optional once the AI gets good enough.

**Rejection test:** *Would this practice hold up if the AI were twice as fast and twice as confident?* If the answer is "we'd probably skip it at that point," the practice is already being treated as a concession to current AI limitations rather than a genuine engineering principle.

---

## Pillar 5 - Context is infrastructure

A stateless collaborator that reads context every session is only as good as the context it reads. Architecture notes, decision records, coding standards, and maintained CLAUDE.md-equivalents are not documentation overhead - they are the mechanism by which a session with no memory behaves like a team member with institutional knowledge. Letting this infrastructure decay is equivalent to letting the test suite decay: the system still runs, but it becomes increasingly unsafe to change.

**What aiXP is not:** a workflow where context is rebuilt from scratch each session by re-explaining what the project is.

**Rejection test:** *Could a fresh session, reading only the maintained context artifacts, make a reasonable implementation decision without asking?* If the answer is consistently no, the context infrastructure has failed this pillar.
